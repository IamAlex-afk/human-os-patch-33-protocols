AI and your thinking

10 questions about how much of your remembering, problem solving and checking you hand over to AI. Answer about the last month — about two minutes. Your answers stay in your browser.

1. Do you ask AI instead of trying to remember something yourself?
2. Do you let AI keep track of facts, dates or names you could remember?
3. Do you forget things soon after AI has told you, because you know you can ask again?
4. Do you ask AI to solve a problem before you have tried yourself?
5. Do you hand a whole task to AI instead of only the part you are stuck on?
6. Do you use AI to make decisions you could make on your own?
7. Do you give up on a hard question quickly because AI can answer it?
8. Do you accept what AI says without checking it?
9. Do you trust AI’s answer more than your own reasoning?
10. Do you pass on AI’s answer as your own without understanding it?

Main test: how much do you rely on AI? 📐 Methodology

About this test

This test shows how much of your thinking you hand over to AI: remembering, solving problems, checking answers. Using AI is fine; what matters is that you keep the habit of thinking for yourself. It is not a medical test and not a diagnosis. Your level is counted only from your own answers, not by comparing you with other people.

More: the research behind the test and how the score is counted

The questions are our own. They follow research on cognitive offloading — using an outside tool for mental work (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) — and two studies of AI use: among 666 people, heavier use of AI tools went with lower critical-thinking scores (Gerlich, 2025), and among 319 knowledge workers, more confidence in generative AI went with less critical thinking (Lee et al., 2025). These are associations, not proof that AI harms thinking. Handing work to a tool is often useful; the test only shows how much you do it. Each answer gives 0–4 points, the total is 0–40; levels: up to 40% of the maximum, 41–70%, above 70% — thresholds from the answer scale, not population norms. The test has not been clinically validated and is not a diagnosis.
