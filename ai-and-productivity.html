AI and productivity

10 questions about habits that can eat up the time AI saves: time spent on AI, the flow of work and unclear benefit. Answer about the last month — about two minutes. Your answers stay in your browser.

1. Do you spend longer rewording requests to AI than the task itself would take?
2. Do you use AI for small tasks that would be quicker to do yourself?
3. Do you drift into conversations with AI that have nothing to do with the task you sat down for?
4. Do you have to redo or fix work because an AI answer was wrong?
5. Do you rely on AI for complex decisions without checking whether it handles them well?
6. Do you put off starting a task until AI has given you a plan?
7. Do you lose your train of thought switching between your work and the AI chat?
8. Do you feel that AI makes you faster without checking whether it really does?
9. Do you end the day with fewer tasks done than planned, even though you used AI a lot?
10. Do you find it hard to say what AI actually saved you this week?

Main test: how much do you rely on AI? 📐 Methodology

About this test

This test shows whether your AI habits really save you time or quietly eat it up: time spent on AI, the flow of your work, and whether the benefit is clear. AI often does speed work up; the test looks for the habits that cancel that out. It is not a medical test and not a diagnosis. Your level is counted only from your own answers, not by comparing you with other people.

More: the research behind the test and how the score is counted

The questions are our own. In experiments, AI often made work faster: professionals finished writing tasks 40% faster and 18% better with ChatGPT (Noy & Zhang, 2023), and support agents solved 15% more requests per hour, most of all the less experienced ones (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025). But the gain depends on the task: consultants with AI did more and faster on tasks AI handles well, and were 19% less likely to be right on a task outside its strengths (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023). In a small 2025 study, experienced developers were 19% slower with AI while believing they were 20% faster (METR; the authors now call this result out of date, and their 2026 follow-up was inconclusive). The test looks at the habits that can eat up the gain. Each answer gives 0–4 points, the total is 0–40; levels: up to 40% of the maximum, 41–70%, above 70% — thresholds from the answer scale, not population norms. The test has not been clinically validated and is not a diagnosis.
